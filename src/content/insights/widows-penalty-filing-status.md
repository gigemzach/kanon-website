Of everything that changes on a tax return after losing a spouse, the change that catches people hardest arrives about two years later, when nothing else about their finances has changed at all.

## The timing

**The year of death.** You can generally still file a joint return for the year your spouse died, provided you did not remarry before year end. For that year, the return looks much like it always has.

**The two following years.** If you have a dependent child living with you and you meet the other conditions, you may file as a qualifying surviving spouse. That status uses the same standard deduction and the same bracket widths as married filing jointly.

**After that.** Single filing status. And if you never had a qualifying dependent child, you land here immediately in the year after death.

## Why it costs money

Single brackets are roughly half as wide as joint brackets at most income levels, and the standard deduction is roughly half as large. The same income runs into higher rates sooner.

The surviving spouse's income, meanwhile, has often not halved. Pensions with survivor benefits continue. Investment income continues, sometimes at a higher level after life insurance proceeds are invested. Required minimum distributions continue from an IRA that may now be larger, having absorbed the deceased spouse's account.

Two other thresholds move at the same time, and they are the ones people rarely see coming:

- **Medicare IRMAA.** The income thresholds for surcharges are lower for single filers. A surviving spouse can pick up a Medicare premium increase driven by income from two years prior — the year their spouse was still alive.
- **Net investment income tax.** The threshold drops from the joint amount to the single amount.

## What can be done, and when

Most of what helps has to happen in the window while joint or qualifying-surviving-spouse rates still apply. That window is short and it is easy to spend grieving.

Things worth examining during it:

- Whether Roth conversions make sense while the wider brackets are still available
- How to sequence realization of gains on assets that received a basis step-up
- Whether to accelerate income or defer deductions across the status change
- The inherited IRA distribution schedule, and how it interacts with the year the brackets narrow

None of this is urgent in the first month. It is genuinely important by the first filing season, and largely gone by the third.

## The practical version

If you have lost a spouse in the last two years, the question worth asking is not "did my return get filed." It is "what does my return look like the year the brackets change, and is there anything I should do before then." That question has an answer, and it is usually worth knowing early.
