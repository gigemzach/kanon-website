A common and unpleasant surprise: your RSUs vested, your employer withheld shares to cover taxes, and you still owe a substantial amount when the return is filed.

Nothing went wrong. The withholding worked exactly as designed. The design just does not match your situation.

## What actually happens at vest

When RSUs vest, the fair market value of the shares is compensation. It runs through payroll and lands in Box 1 of your W-2, the same as salary.

Employers generally withhold federal income tax on that supplemental wage income at a **flat statutory rate**, not at your marginal rate. For supplemental wages up to $1 million in a year, that flat rate is 22%. Above $1 million, the excess is withheld at 37%.

## Where the gap comes from

If your marginal federal rate is 32%, 35%, or 37% — which it likely is, if you have meaningful equity income — then 22% withholding leaves a shortfall of ten to fifteen percentage points on every dollar of vested stock.

On $200,000 of vesting, a 13-point gap is roughly $26,000 owed at filing, on income you watched shares get withheld against. Add the 3.8% net investment income tax and state tax if you have one, and the number grows.

The mechanism that normally protects people from this — the withholding on regular salary being calibrated to their overall situation — does not extend to supplemental wages. Payroll is not looking at your total picture.

## The second problem: basis

If you sold shares at or near vest, your broker will report the sale on Form 1099-B. That form frequently shows a cost basis of zero, or omits basis entirely, because the broker was never told what you already paid tax on.

Reported as-is, you pay ordinary income tax on the vest **and** capital gains tax on the full proceeds of the sale. The same dollars, taxed twice.

The fix is a basis adjustment on Form 8949 — your basis is the amount already included as compensation. It is straightforward once someone knows to look. It is also one of the most frequently missed items on high-income returns, and it is a leading cause of CP2000 notices, because the IRS matching system sees the 1099-B and not the correction.

## What to do about it

**If vesting is ahead of you.** Estimate the shortfall and cover it, either by increasing withholding on regular wages through your W-4 or by making a quarterly estimated payment. Increasing W-4 withholding is generally the cleaner route, because withholding is treated as paid evenly across the year regardless of when it actually occurred, which helps with underpayment penalties.

**If vesting already happened.** Work out the number now rather than in April. The safe-harbor rules can eliminate underpayment penalties if you act before year end, and they cannot help you afterward.

**Either way,** check the basis on any equity sale before the return is filed, and check prior-year returns too. If the adjustment was missed, an amended return is often worth filing.
